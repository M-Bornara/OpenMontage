## Timeline

| before 1848 | Harlow: "a perfectly healthy, strong and active young man", "possessing an iron will as well as an iron frame". His employers thought him "the most efficient and capable foreman in their employ". | P1 |
| 13 Sep 1848 | Rutland & Burlington Railroad cut near **Cavendish, Vermont**. Gage, **25**, foreman of a blasting gang, is tamping powder when it explodes. The iron (**3 ft 7 in, 1¼ in diameter, 13¼ lb**, pointed at one end) enters below the left cheekbone, passes behind the left eye, and exits the top of the skull. It lands about 80 ft (25 m) away. | P1 pp.4–5; S1 |
| minutes later | "Spoke in a few minutes". His men carry him to an **ox cart**, and he rides "supported in a sitting posture" ¾ mile to his hotel. He gets out "with a little assistance" and walks up a long flight of stairs. | P1 pp.5–6 |
| ~30 min | To Dr. Edward Williams: **"Doctor, here is business enough for you."** | Williams, via P2 / S1 |
| 1848–49 | A severe infection, then recovery under Harlow | P1 |
| 1849 | Harlow: "fitful, irreverent, indulging at times in the grossest profanity… impatient of restraint"; his friends say he is **"no longer Gage"**. The contractors won't give him his old job back. | P1 p.13–14 |
| 1849–50 | Shown to the Boston Society for Medical Improvement. Spends time at **Barnum's** museum in New York "with his iron". | P1 p.14; S1 |
| ~1849 | Gives his iron to Harvard's Warren Museum, then **takes it back**. It becomes "my iron bar", "his constant companion during the remainder of his life". | P1, S1 |
| 1851–52 | Works about 18 months at a livery stable in Hanover, NH | P1 p.14 |
| Aug 1852 | Goes to **Chile** to help set up a coach line at Valparaíso. For about 7 years he drives "a coach heavily laden and drawn by **six horses**" on the Valparaíso–Santiago route. | P1 p.14; S1 |
| 1859–60 | His health fails. He goes to his mother in San Francisco and works on a farm in Santa Clara. Seizures begin in Feb 1860. | P1 pp.14–16 |
| 21 May 1860 | Dies of seizures (status epilepticus), aged about 36. Harlow wrongly gives 1861. | S1 |
| 1867–68 | At Harlow's request, his family has the **skull exhumed** and brings it and the iron to Harlow. Both are now in the **Warren Anatomical Museum**, Harvard. | P1; S1 |
| 2009 / 2010 | Two portraits of Gage holding the iron are identified. They "exploded the common image of Gage as a dirty, disheveled misfit". | S3; L1 |

## Myth vs record
| Popular version | Record |
|---|---|
| He became a violent, drunken, dishonest drifter who couldn't hold a job and died penniless in an institution | Macmillan: accounts "almost always distort and exaggerate" beyond anyone with direct contact. The known facts are "inconsistent with the common view of Gage as a boastful, brawling, foul-mouthed, dishonest useless drifter". (S1, via L1) |
| The change was permanent | One leading interpretation (S2): he made "a surprisingly good psychosocial adaptation". The daily structure of coach work may have helped. **Say "one leading interpretation", not "proven".** |
| A dramatic personality change | Harlow did report real early changes ("no longer Gage"). Both things are true: an early change, and a later partial recovery. |
| He had a wife and family | No spouse and no children (S1) |
| "the American crowbar case" | It was a smooth tamping iron, not a crowbar (P1) |

## Human angles
A proud foreman who lost his job, then reinvented himself on another continent. He loved children, horses
and dogs, and told his nephews and nieces wild stories (P1). He kept the iron that nearly killed him, and
even got it back from a museum. He died young, far from home, and doctors dug up his skull.

## Advertiser safety
- Never show the rod in a head as gore. Use a clean anatomical diagram (skull outline + trajectory line), or
  the portrait holding the iron.
- Avoid "smeared with blood and brain" (P1).
