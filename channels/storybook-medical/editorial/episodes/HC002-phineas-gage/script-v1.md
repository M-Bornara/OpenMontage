## S1 — An Iron Rod Went Through His Skull
`[excited]` In 1848, an iron rod shot straight through a man's skull… and he sat up and talked. [C04–C05]
Phineas Gage. Twenty-five. Railroad foreman in Vermont. [C01]
He was packing blasting powder into a rock with an iron bar. More than a meter long. Six kilos. [C02]
Then the powder went off. [C03]
The bar went in under his left cheekbone… behind his eye… and out through the top of his head. It landed about twenty-five meters away. [C04]
`[curious]` Within minutes… Phineas was talking. His men put him in an ox cart, and he rode into town sitting up. [C05]
When the doctor arrived, Phineas greeted him: "Doctor, here is business enough for you." [C06]
He survived. He even kept the iron bar… for the rest of his life. [C07]
`[whispers]` But his friends said he was "no longer Gage." [C08]

## S2 — Did Phineas Gage Really Become a Different Person?
`[curious]` You've probably heard this story. An iron rod went through Phineas Gage's brain… and turned a good man into a monster. [C09]
The records tell a stranger story.
Yes, at first his doctor called him "fitful," "irreverent," swearing. His friends said he was "no longer Gage." [C08]
But here's what the famous version skips.
`[excited]` Gage moved to Chile. For about seven years, he drove stagecoaches. Six horses. Heavy coaches. On the road between Valparaíso and Santiago. [C10]
That's not a man who lost control of his life.
Historian Malcolm Macmillan argues Gage may have largely recovered… and that the routine of the job helped him relearn how to live with people. [C11]
In 2009, a portrait surfaced: Gage, well dressed… proudly holding his iron. [C12]
`[whispers]` The monster? Mostly added later. [C09]

*~120 words. Visual idea: a "textbook page" drawing of a snarling Gage that tears away to reveal the six-horse
coach on a Chilean road; a map from Vermont to Valparaíso.*
