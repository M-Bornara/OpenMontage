# HC003 — The Dancing Plague of 1518 · research

Researched 2026-10-18. **Uncertainty is part of this story.** We rely on chronicles written during and after
1518. Several were compiled decades or centuries later, and the leading scholar's interpretation is not
proven.

## Sources
- **S1** — Waller J. *A Time to Dance, a Time to Die: The Extraordinary Story of the Dancing Plague of
  1518.* Icon, 2008. The leading scholarly treatment. US edition: *The Dancing Plague*, Sourcebooks, 2009.
- **S2** — Waller J. "A forgotten plague: making sense of dancing mania." *Lancet* 2009;373(9664):624–5.
  doi:10.1016/S0140-6736(09)60386-X
- **S3** — Midelfort HCE. *A History of Madness in Sixteenth-Century Germany.* Stanford UP, 1999. Quotes the
  Specklin chronicle.
- **S4** — Pennant-Rea N. "The Dancing Plague of 1518." *Public Domain Review*, 2018. Synthesises S1 and
  quotes the chronicles (Specklin, the Imlin family chronicle, Brant's annals, Paracelsus, the 1636
  chronicle). https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/the-dancing-plague-of-1518/
- **L1** — Wikipedia, "Dancing plague of 1518". A lead source; useful for flagging the death-count dispute.

Primary-derived voices (quoted through S1, S3 and S4):
- Daniel Specklin's chronicle: the council's stages and musicians.
- The Imlin family chronicle: 400 afflicted.
- Sebastian Brant's *Annales*: the music ban and its exceptions.
- Paracelsus, *Opus Paramirum*: Frau Troffea's story.
- An astrological chronicle of 1636: dancing "until finally they fell down unconscious and succumbed to death".

## What happened (with confidence levels)
| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| A dancing epidemic hit **Strasbourg** (then in the Holy Roman Empire, today France), from **mid-July to late August or early September 1518** | VERIFIED (multiple chronicles, council notes) |
| It began with one woman, **Frau Troffea**, on about **14 July**, dancing alone in the street with no music | ATTRIBUTED (Paracelsus; 4 of 6 chronicle accounts name her) |
| She danced for **4–6 days**, collapsing and starting again; her husband pleaded with her to stop | ATTRIBUTED |
| Within days, **30+** people were dancing | ATTRIBUTED (S4) |
| The physicians declared it "a natural disease, which comes from overheated blood". The cure: **dance it out**. | ATTRIBUTED (council/physicians via S4) |
| The council had guild halls turned into dance floors, built **platforms in the horse market and grain market**, and paid **musicians** (drums, pipes, horns) and strong men to keep dancers upright | ATTRIBUTED (Specklin chronicle via S3/S4) |
| It **backfired**; within a month **400** were afflicted | ATTRIBUTED (Imlin chronicle). Elsewhere estimates run 50–400, so DISPUTED. |
| The council tore down the stages, moved dancers out of sight, and **banned public music and dancing until September**. At weddings, stringed instruments only, "not… tambourines and drums". | ATTRIBUTED (Brant's *Annales* via S1/S4) |
| The worst cases went by wagon to the **shrine of St. Vitus near Saverne**: red shoes, crosses, holy water. The outbreak faded. | ATTRIBUTED (S1/S4) |
| **Deaths** ("up to 15 a day") | **DISPUTED.** Strasbourg's own records of the time don't mention deaths. The death claims come from later chronicles (L1; S4). Say: "Later chronicles claimed people died. The city's own records don't say so." |

## Explanations (never present one as proven)
| Explanation | Status |
|---|---|
| A punishment from **St. Vitus** | The belief of many at the time |
| "Overheated blood" | The physicians' humoral diagnosis at the time |
| Paracelsus: Frau Troffea was faking it to annoy her husband, and other women copied her | His interpretation, 8 years later. A good humour beat; clearly not accepted today. |
| **Ergot poisoning** (a mould on rye) | Popular but contested. Waller: ergot restricts blood flow, so victims "could not have danced for days at a time". It also can't explain why outbreaks clustered along the Rhine and Moselle. |
| **Mass psychogenic illness** (stress-induced, shaped by belief in St. Vitus): famine, disease, poverty, and earlier dancing outbreaks nearby | **One leading interpretation** (Waller, S1/S2). There were more than 10 such outbreaks along the Rhine and Moselle from 1374. |
| Modern parallels: the Tanganyika laughter epidemic (began 1962; some accounts say 1963) | Context only |

## Human angles
- A woman alone in a street.
- Hungry, frightened people.
- A city government trying to help and making it worse.
- Faith and fear.
- Mercy at the shrine (red shoes).

## Advertiser safety
- Avoid bloody feet close-ups (chronicles mention them). Use exhaustion, silhouettes and crowds.
- Don't show deaths. If mentioned at all, they appear only as a disputed claim.
