# Oddly Matt — cinematography language

Our films are made from illustrated stills, a few 5-second clips and camera moves in Remotion, so the
"camera" is mostly *framing, layering and movement*. These rules make every shot mean something, which is
what separates cinematic storytelling from a slideshow.

## 1. Shot sizes and what they're for
| Size | Use it for | Never for |
|---|---|---|
| **Extreme wide (EWS)** | place, scale, loneliness (a tiny figure in a big world) | emotion |
| **Wide (WS)** | geography: who is where, before a scene starts | punchlines |
| **Medium (MS)** | action and conversation | the hook (too neutral) |
| **Close-up (CU)** | emotion and decisions ("Alexis said NO") | information |
| **Extreme close-up / insert (ECU)** | the object that carries the story (string, watch, iron bar, red shoes, catheter coil) | faces in pain, wounds |

**Hooks open on an ECU or a striking EWS, never a medium.**

## 2. Angles
- **Eye level:** empathy (the default for our protagonists).
- **Low angle:** power or defiance (Alexis refusing; Gage the foreman; Marshall drinking).
- **High angle:** vulnerability or smallness (Troffea alone in the street; Bågenholm's skis in the ice).
- **Over-the-shoulder / point of view:** discovery, putting the viewer in someone's eyes (Beaumont looking at
  the opening; Forssmann's X-ray).
- **Dutch tilt:** only for a world going wrong (the dancing spreading), and at most once per film.

## 3. Camera moves: motivated, one per shot
| Move | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Push in** | realisation, rising tension. Used on the line that turns the story. |
| **Pull out** | reveal, consequence, isolation ("He lived with that opening for 58 years") |
| **Slide / pan** | journey, connection between two things, time passing |
| **Tilt** | scale (up the iron's flight, down to the ice hole) |
| **Whip** | time jump or a sudden turn (only as a transition) |
| **Shake** | impact only (musket, explosion, ice breaking), 3–6 frames |
| **Static** | comedy and deadpan. Stillness lands a joke ("Medical ethics committees were not exactly a thing yet.") |

## 4. Composition
- Thirds by default; centre only for symmetry with a purpose (Forssmann's long corridor, Stockholm hall).
- **Three depth layers** (foreground, midground, background) in every wide shot, for parallax.
- **Frames within frames:** doorways, windows and wagon openings, for confinement and watching.
- **Negative space** for loneliness (Troffea, Bågenholm's white world).
- **Shorts safe zone:** faces and key objects stay out of the bottom 22% and the right 12%, where the
  TikTok/Shorts UI sits, and the top 10% for the status bar.

## 5. Continuity
- **180° rule:** in a two-person scene, each person keeps their side. Beaumont is always screen-left and
  Alexis screen-right.
- **Screen direction:** journeys away move left to right, and returns move right to left (Alexis to Canada →
  L to R; brought back → R to L). The same goes for maps.
- **Eyelines:** a cut from a look must land on what is looked at.
- **Recurring motifs** (one per story, seen in the hook and again in the payoff):
  - St. Martin: the **pocket watch** and the **silk string**.
  - Gage: the **iron bar**.
  - Dancing Plague: the **red shoes**.
  - Forssmann: the **catheter coil**.
  - Marshall: the **beaker**.
  - Bågenholm: the **thermometer**.

## 6. Cutting
- **Cut on sound:** the musket crack, the iron's spark, the drum hit. The picture changes on the
  transient.
- **Match cuts** link ideas: a string pulled out → the next hour's string; a log split → a skull diagram
  split line.
- **Pacing curve per Short:**
  - **0–7 s:** fast, a cut every 1–1.5 s.
  - **Escalation:** 1.5–2.5 s per cut.
  - **The "WAIT…" line:** hold one beat longer (silence works).
  - **Reveal:** fastest cutting.
  - **Final line:** hold 2 s on the payoff image.
- One strong still gives 2–3 beats: wide → punch-in crop → insert. Change the *picture* every 1.5–3 s; don't
  move the camera faster.
- **Transitions:**
  - **cut** is the default;
  - **whip** for time jumps;
  - **white flash** for violence (we never show the violent frame itself);
  - **dissolve** only for the passing of years.

## 7. Light and colour as story
Each story has its own palette inside our house style. The palette shifts with the emotional turn.

| Story | Base | Turn |
|---|---|---|
| St. Martin | amber trading post, firelight | teal sickroom → warm again at survival |
| Gage | dusty ochre, autumn Vermont | cold whisper-grey on "no longer Gage" → bright Chilean sun in S2 |
| Dancing Plague | midsummer gold, heat haze | dusk purples as it spreads; candle-gold at the shrine |
| Forssmann | 1929 hospital: cool green-white + one amber lamp | X-ray blue glow |
| Marshall | 1980s Perth sun + fluorescent lab teal | sickly green on the illness → Stockholm gold |
| Bågenholm | white and ice-blue | **cold → warm** in the hospital (the colour *is* the story) |

## 8. Restraint (advertiser-safe)
- Violence is shown by **cause → white flash → aftermath**, never the impact.
- Wounds become bandages, silhouettes, reaction shots or clean diagrams.
- Medical procedures show the **tool and the face**, not skin being pierced.
- Diagrams are clean, soft-coloured and educational: anatomy in pastel pink and teal, no blood.

## 9. Fun on screen
- **Visual comedy** through deadpan holds, repetition and escalation:
  - the string coming out emptier each hour;
  - the band playing harder as more people dance;
  - the letters piling up.
- **One playful device per Short at most**, e.g. a look to camera, an item slamming into frame, or a stamp.
- **The joke is always on the situation** or the era, never on the person.

## 10. The fun layer: props, wardrobe and set dressing
Every Short gets 3–5 **eye-catching details** that reward a second look without touching the story.

**Rules**
1. **Real but weird beats invented.** The best props are true to the period and surprising today (a hurdy-gurdy,
   a leech jar, a Barnum's poster, a 1929 X-ray machine). Anything else must be *plausible* for the place and
   year. **No anachronisms:**
   - no dynamite before 1867 (Gage used blasting powder);
   - no aurora in a Norwegian May (it's light all night);
   - no mobile phones in 1984 Perth.
2. **Background and foreground layers only.** The subject and the line's key image stay clean. Fun items sit
   at the edges or in the depth, so the eye finds them on a second look.
3. **Reveal on holds.** Put the funniest detail in the deadpan or static shots, where the eye has time to
   wander.
4. **Props make no claims.** No invented documents, labels or inscriptions that read like facts. Text on props
   is either real (from our sources) or generic.
5. **Never at anyone's expense.** Animals and onlookers may react to the absurd situation, but never to
   anyone's suffering.
6. **Continuity.** Once introduced, a prop or wardrobe signature stays (Beaumont's watch, Forssmann's one
   rolled-up sleeve).
7. **The red rule.** Saturated red belongs to each story's motif (Alexis's tuque, the red shoes). Other props
   stay out of pure red, so the motif still pops.
8. **One hidden "spot it" item per film: Matt's spectacles.** These are round wire spectacles with amber lenses
   and a small "?" glint, left somewhere in every scene set. A long video may also include **one cameo** of
   Matt, the time-travelling onlooker (teal scarf, amber notebook, era-appropriate clothes). Both are defined
   in `plan/brand-signature.md`. Neither is ever in the story's way.
