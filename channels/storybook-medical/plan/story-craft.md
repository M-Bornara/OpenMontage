# Oddly Matt — story craft (v3.2, 2026-10-18)

How we pick and write stories. It sits on top of the master instructions; the fact rules there still apply
to every line.

## Identity in one paragraph
Fast, cinematic, beautifully illustrated true stories about extraordinary human experiences from history,
medicine, science and survival.
- Every story starts with an almost unbelievable premise, follows a real person through escalating events,
  and ends with a satisfying discovery, consequence, twist or correction.
- Liam narrates everything.
- Shorts are complete 35–55 s mini-stories built on one angle.
- Long videos are complete ~5–10 min narratives with more character, context, conflict and aftermath.
- Both come from one research universe, and each has its own reason to exist.
- Never pad, never invent, never fake a cliffhanger, never trade entertainment for sounding educational.

**Promise:** stories too strange to sound true — except they actually happened.
**Real subject:** humans in extraordinary situations. Medicine, history, science and survival are lenses.
**Brand test:** "Is this oddly human?" Does it make people say "wait… that actually happened?", and can our
research answer "yes, and here's what the evidence says"?

## The emotional engine
**WHAT?!** (impossible premise) → **HOW?!** (needs explaining) → **WAIT…** (it gets complicated) → **WOW.**
(something worth remembering)

## Picking topics
1. **Thumbnail sentence test.** Can you say why it's fascinating in one sentence? ("An iron rod passed
   through his skull and he survived.") If not, reject it. If the title doesn't almost write itself, the
   story is probably weak.
2. **People, not facts.** Generic science questions ("why doesn't your stomach digest itself?") belong
   inside a human story, not as stand-alone videos; thousands of channels already make those.
   - This replaces v3.1's "Curiosity Shorts" type: curiosity always comes through a person.
3. **Weighted score (1–5).** WTF factor 20% · human character 15% · story progression 15% · visual
   potential 15% · evidence quality 15% · surprise/twist 10% · global appeal 5% · brand fit 5%.
   - ≥ 4.0: produce. 3.0–3.9: research more. < 3.0: don't spend credits.
4. **Classify after the Story Expansion Map:**
   - **S** (Short only): one excellent idea.
   - **L** (Long only): loses too much when compressed.
   - **U** (Story Universe): one long + 2–3 standalone Shorts. This is the best kind; actively hunt for these.

   Never promote a story to long-form just because the topic is interesting; it needs enough turns.

## Shorts (35–55 s, never forced)
| Time | Job | Example (St. Martin, "food on a string") |
|---|---|---|
| 0–1.5 s | Impossible premise. No intro, no logo, no "did you know" | "A doctor once lowered food directly into a living man's stomach." |
| 1.5–7 s | Just enough who/where/why | "Alexis St. Martin had survived a gunshot that left an opening into his stomach." |
| 7–25 s | Escalate with **because → therefore → but → so**; every sentence causes the next | Beaumont saw a chance no doctor ever had → tied food to strings → lowered it in → pulled it out later |
| 25–40 s | Reveal: what happened, what they found, what changed | what came out of the stomach (claim-checked) |
| 40–50 s | Bigger curiosity *after* the answer | "And that was only one of hundreds of experiments." |

No fake cliffhangers, ever. The long video offers *more story*, not the missing ending.

## Long videos (~5–10 min, the shortest version that feels complete)
**The first 30 s plays like a Short:** fast cuts, the strangest moment first, then the title.

Default spine (not every story needs all ten):
1. Impossible opening
2. Who is this person?
3. How did it happen?
4. It gets stranger
5. The obvious explanation isn't enough
6. Discovery or conflict
7. What happened next
8. What people remember
9. What the evidence actually says
10. Why it matters

**A mini-hook every 30–90 s**, as a real narrative turn and never bait:
- "But there was a problem."
- "Then St. Martin disappeared."
- "That's where the famous version starts falling apart."

## Writing for Liam
- **Persona:** your extremely curious friend who just found an unbelievable true story and has to tell you.
  Not a professor, news anchor, trailer voice or TikTok hype machine.
- **Short sentences, mixed rhythm:** short, medium, very short.
  - "Alexis should have died." / "He didn't." / "And that created a problem nobody expected."
  - "Beaumont tried something strange. He tied a piece of food to a string and lowered it through the
    opening. Then he waited."
- **Humour** comes from absurd situations, timing and visual irony, plus an occasional dry line ("Medical
  ethics committees were not exactly a thing yet."). Never laugh at the person, their suffering, death,
  disability, body, ethnicity or appearance. St. Martin is never the joke.

## Visuals
- Keep the hand-painted 2.5D look, but make it energetic: parallax, pushes, pans, foreground movement,
  light changes, particles, maps, documents, diagrams, silhouettes, object close-ups.
- **One strong generated scene → many beats:** wide, push-in, character crop, object close-up, light change.
  Don't generate a new image every 2 s.
- **Let images carry information.** If letters stack up on screen, Liam doesn't also say "he wrote many
  letters". Visual comedy is welcome when the fact is documented (e.g. Beaumont's letters piling up,
  only if the correspondence supports "persistent").

## The content formula (lock)
1. Find something unbelievable that actually happened.
2. Find the human being inside it.
3. Identify the central question.
4. Research what actually happened.
5. Find where it gets stranger.
6. Find what later retellings got wrong.
7. Build a fast narrative of cause and consequence.
8. Let Liam tell it like an incredible story, not a lecture.
9. Use cinematic illustration to put the viewer there.
10. Give a satisfying answer.
11. Leave them curious about another story, never frustrated by a fake cliffhanger.

## Season Zero (first 90 days) — let the audience choose
- Universes: St. Martin, Phineas Gage and the Dancing Plague.
- Independent Shorts: Forssmann and Marshall.
- Next universe if research supports it: Anna Bågenholm (survival).
- Read the results by family, not by video. Examples:
  - If Gage and the Dancing Plague win → myths and historical mysteries.
  - If survival wins → Koepcke, extreme cold, shipwrecks, rescues.
  - If self-experimenters win → a story universe built on one question ("why would a scientist risk their
    own life to prove an idea?"), not a listicle.
- **Growth path:** topic → thumbnail → title → first 10 s → story → ending → brand. We don't try to hack
  the algorithm.
  - After a Short: "that was crazy".
  - After a long video: "that was actually a great story".
  - After ten videos: "I watch these whenever they upload".
