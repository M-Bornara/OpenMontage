**Title:** The Enslaved Man Who Saved Boston

**Description:**
```
1721, Boston. Smallpox swept through the town. Years earlier, Onesimus, an enslaved West African man in the household of the minister Cotton Mather, had told Mather how people in West Africa protected themselves: a tiny cut, a little matter from a smallpox sore, a mild illness, then protection for life.

Mather begged the town's doctors to try it. Only Zabdiel Boylston agreed. On 26 June 1721 he inoculated his own six-year-old son and two enslaved people in his household. The town was furious: in November someone threw a grenade through Mather's window, and the fuse went out. The note began: "Cotton Mather, you dog, damn you! I'll inoculate you with this..."

About 1 in 7 people who caught smallpox in Boston that year died (844 of about 5,759). Among Boylston's inoculated patients, about 1 in 40 (6 of 247). Inoculation still carried real risk; vaccination, safer, came 75 years later. The idea came from Onesimus, and for centuries his name was mostly left out of the story. This is a history story, not medical advice.

More true medical stories: "The Man With a Window Into His Stomach" (linked above).
```
